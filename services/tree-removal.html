Getting Started

When Tree Removal Becomes Necessary

Removal is always the last recommendation, not the first. Every job Prime Tree Care evaluates starts with an on-site inspection by an ISA-certified arborist to determine whether a tree can be treated, pruned, or supported — and only moves to removal when the tree presents a genuine safety or structural risk that can't be corrected.

Chicago's mature tree canopy, clay-heavy soil, and increasingly severe storm seasons combine to create specific failure patterns our crews see repeatedly. Dead trees lose the structural integrity that living wood provides, and once a tree is fully dead, limb and trunk failure becomes a matter of when, not if. Advanced decay — often invisible from the outside — hollows out a trunk or major limb from within, and by the time fungal conks or soft, spongy wood are visible at the base, the tree may already be unsafe to climb.

Structural failure shows up as included bark, co-dominant stems splitting apart, or a canopy that has grown unevenly heavy on one side. Storm damage from Chicago's derechos, straight-line winds, and ice events can snap major limbs or crack a trunk in ways that aren't repairable. A severe lean that has developed suddenly, especially paired with lifted soil or exposed roots, often signals root failure — the tree's anchoring system giving way, which puts it at real risk of a full uproot in the next high-wind event.

Utility conflicts arise when a tree's canopy or root system grows into overhead lines or underground infrastructure, creating both a safety hazard and a maintenance conflict that a utility company will eventually force a resolution on. And irreversible disease — Dutch elm disease in mature elms or late-stage emerald ash borer infestation in ash trees, for example — reaches a point where treatment can no longer save the tree and removal protects the surrounding, still-healthy trees on the property.

In every one of these cases, our approach is the same: inspect first, treat and prune where it's genuinely possible, and only recommend removal when it's the responsible, safety-driven decision. If you're unsure which category your tree falls into, our tree health inspection and treatment service is the right starting point before any removal is scheduled.

Is My Tree Dangerous?

Signs a Tree May Be Hazardous

These are the warning signs our arborists look for first during any hazard evaluation. If your tree shows one or more of these signs, it's worth scheduling an inspection before the next storm.

1

Large Dead Limbs

Dead branches lose flexibility and can fail without warning, even in light wind.

2

Hollow Trunk Sections

Hollowing reduces the wood available to bear the tree's own weight and wind load.

3

Fungal Growth at the Base

Conks and mushrooms at the root flare often indicate internal decay already underway.

4

Major Trunk Cracks

Vertical or horizontal cracks can signal the trunk is under stress and close to failure.

5

Splitting Stems

Co-dominant trunks with included bark are prone to splitting apart under weight or wind.

6

Root Plate Movement

Soil heaving or cracking near the base suggests the root system is losing its grip.

7

Significant Lean

A new or worsening lean, especially after a storm, is one of the clearest uproot risks.

8

Canopy Dieback

Thinning or dying foliage starting at the crown often points to root or vascular stress.

9

Storm Damage

Broken limbs, stripped bark, or a cracked leader from a recent storm need prompt review.

10

Previous Branch Failures

A tree that has already dropped limbs is statistically more likely to drop more.

11

Lightning Strikes

Strike damage can kill cambium tissue and destabilize a tree even without visible scarring.

12

Decay Cavities

Open cavities in the trunk or major limbs reduce structural strength at that point.

Specialized Equipment

Crane-Assisted Tree Removal

Some trees simply can't be removed safely with climbing and rigging alone. When a tree is too large, too close to a structure, or positioned in a tight-access yard, a crane becomes the safest and most efficient option.

Cranes are typically necessary for large mature trees with heavy limb sections, properties with limited access for a bucket truck, and trees growing directly over a house, garage, or power line where controlled, section-by-section lowering is the only safe method. The safety advantage is significant: instead of a climber cutting and dropping sections through the canopy, the crane lifts each piece cleanly away from the work zone, which also means faster project completion and dramatically reduced risk of property damage to roofs, fences, gardens, and neighboring structures.

Our crews are trained on crane rental coordination, bucket truck operation, and aerial lift access, and we select the right equipment for each job rather than defaulting to the cheapest option for a removal that genuinely calls for more capability.

Permit Guidance

Do You Need a Permit to Remove a Tree in Chicago?

Short answer: it depends on who owns the tree. Private trees on your own property generally do not require a City of Chicago permit to remove. Parkway trees — planted in the strip between the sidewalk and the street — are owned by the City and require going through the Bureau of Forestry rather than a private contractor alone.

Private trees anywhere on your own lot are your responsibility to maintain, insure, and remove as needed, though some suburbs outside Chicago proper apply their own diameter-based permit thresholds, so it's worth checking local ordinance before a large removal. Parkway trees fall under the Bureau of Forestry, the City department responsible for planting, maintaining, and removing trees in the public right-of-way. Requests for parkway tree inspection or removal typically go through CHI311, Chicago's non-emergency service request system, rather than a direct permit application.

City-owned trees on park land or other public property follow a similar path through the relevant city department, and easements and right-of-way issues can complicate removal when a tree sits near a shared property line, utility easement, or drainage easement — situations where Prime Tree Care can help document the tree's condition and location before any removal request is filed.

After the Storm

Insurance Claims After Tree Damage

Whether homeowner's insurance covers a tree removal usually depends on what the tree damaged, not just that it fell.

When a fallen tree or storm damage impacts a covered structure — your house, garage, fence, or shed — homeowner insurance typically covers removal and repair, minus your deductible. A tree that falls in the yard without damaging a structure is handled less consistently across policies, so it's worth reviewing your specific coverage. Documentation matters immediately after any storm event: photos of the tree, the damage, and the surrounding property from multiple angles support a faster, cleaner claim.

An arborist report documenting the tree's condition, likely cause of failure, and removal scope can strengthen a claim, especially for larger losses. Where a fallen tree creates an ongoing hazard — blocking an entrance, resting against a structure, or leaning toward a second target — emergency mitigation removal is often covered even before a full claim is finalized, since insurers generally expect reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Liability considerations also come into play when a tree falls onto a neighboring property; in most cases, each property owner's own insurance covers the damage on their side of the line, regardless of whose tree caused it, though the specifics can vary.

If you're filing an insurance claim (fallen branch) or a full tree loss, our crews can provide the documentation and tree risk assessment your adjuster will likely request.

Common Questions

Tree Removal FAQs

Straight answers to the questions Chicago property owners ask us most about tree removal.

How much does tree removal cost in Chicago?

Costs typically range from $300–$800 for small trees, $1,500–$3,000 for medium trees, and $6,000–$9,000+ for large trees, depending on height, diameter, access, crane needs, and disposal.

Do I need a permit to remove a tree?

Private trees on your own property generally don't require a city permit. Parkway trees are owned by the City of Chicago and go through the Bureau of Forestry via CHI311.

What is a parkway tree?

A parkway tree grows in the strip between the sidewalk and the street and is owned and maintained by the City of Chicago's Bureau of Forestry, not the adjacent homeowner.

Is emergency tree removal available?

Yes. Prime Tree Care provides 24/7 emergency response for storm damage, fallen limbs, and trees threatening a structure across Chicago and the suburbs.

Can a tree be removed near power lines?

Yes, with specialized rigging and, when necessary, coordination with the utility company. This work should never be attempted without trained, insured crews.

What is crane-assisted tree removal?

A crane lifts and lowers cut sections of a large tree in a controlled way, reducing property damage risk and often making removal faster and safer than climbing alone.

Does insurance cover storm-damaged trees?

Often, when the tree damages a covered structure. Coverage for a tree that falls without hitting a structure varies by policy — documentation and an arborist report help support a claim.

How do I know if my tree is dangerous?

Watch for large dead limbs, a hollow trunk, fungal growth at the base, deep cracks, significant lean, root movement, or canopy dieback — any of these warrants an arborist inspection.

Is it cheaper to trim a tree or remove it?

Trimming is usually less expensive, but a tree with irreversible decay or major structural failure is a poor candidate for pruning — removal becomes the safer long-term option.

Can an arborist tell if a tree should be removed?

Yes. An ISA-certified arborist evaluates species, structural integrity, decay, and root condition to determine whether treatment or removal is the right path.
