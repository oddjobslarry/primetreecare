The Case for Removal

Why Remove a Tree Stump After Tree Removal?

A leftover stump isn't just an eyesore — it's a lasting trip hazard, a magnet for wood-boring pests, and a barrier to whatever you want to do with that part of your yard next. Most Chicago homeowners choose to grind the stump immediately after tree removal so the job is genuinely finished, not half-done.

Safety Hazards

An exposed stump sits at exactly the wrong height to catch a foot, a mower blade, or a bike tire. On properties with kids, older adults, or renters walking the yard after dark, a stump is a liability sitting in plain sight — and if someone gets hurt on your property, you're the one who answers for it.

Landscape Appearance

A dead stump surrounded by dying grass is one of the fastest ways to drag down curb appeal, especially on a corner lot or a property you're preparing to list. Grinding it out and leveling the site lets your lawn read as intentional again, not like a project someone abandoned halfway.

Pest Concerns

Decaying stumps are a preferred nesting site for carpenter ants, wood-boring beetles, and fungal decay organisms — all of which can spread to nearby healthy trees, fences, or even a house's wood siding if the stump sits close enough to the structure.

Future Property Use

Whether you're planning to replant, lay new sod, pour a patio, or extend a driveway, a ground stump is a prerequisite. Root mass left in the ground will interfere with excavation, grading, and root systems of anything planted nearby.

The Process, Explained

What Is Stump Grinding?

Stump grinding is the mechanical removal of a tree stump using a specialized grinding wheel that chews the wood down into wood chips, well below the surrounding soil grade — typically 6–12 inches below ground. It's faster, less disruptive to the surrounding landscape, and considerably less expensive than full excavation.

Our crews use track-mounted or towable stump grinders sized to the job — compact units for tight backyard gates, larger machines for open-access commercial sites. The grinder works in controlled passes across the stump face and outward across the root flare, reducing the wood to chips as it goes. We grind below grade rather than just flush with the surface, remove the root collar enough to prevent regrowth and interference with new plantings, and finish by backfilling the cavity with a mix of the ground wood chips and topsoil so the site is level and ready for grass seed, sod, or replanting.

Planning Ahead

Can You Plant a New Tree After Stump Grinding?

Yes — in most cases you can plant a new tree in the same general area once the stump is ground and the site is prepped, though the exact spot, soil amendment, and species matter more than most homeowners expect.

Soil Conditions

Wood chips and residual decaying material change soil chemistry as they break down. We typically recommend blending in fresh topsoil and letting the site settle before planting directly over the old root zone.

Species Selection

Some species handle replanting into a former root zone better than others. We'll walk you through what to plant instead of ash if the removed tree was an EAB casualty, since diversifying species reduces future disease risk.

Root Competition

Ground stump material decomposes over a year or two but old root mass below grinding depth can still compete with a new tree's roots for water and nutrients nearby.

Replacement Tree Planning

Our arborists help you choose placement, timing, and species for a tree planting and transplanting project that actually thrives in Chicago's clay soil.

Homeowner Myths, Answered

Do Tree Roots Continue Growing After Stump Grinding?

No — once the stump and root flare are ground out, the tree can no longer grow. However, root sections left in the soil below grinding depth don't disappear immediately; they decay gradually over one to several years, and some species can send up suckers from surviving root fragments until that decay is complete.

Root Decay Process

Remaining underground roots break down slowly, feeding soil organisms as they go — this is a normal, harmless process, not a sign the job was done incorrectly.

Remaining Root Systems

Large lateral roots can extend well beyond the grinding radius; they won't resprout a new trunk but may remain physically present for years.

Suckering Potential

Species like silver maple and willow are notorious for sending up root suckers after removal — we flag this during the site inspection so you know what to expect.

Species Differences

Some species stop sending shoots almost immediately after grinding; others need a season or two of monitoring and occasional follow-up mowing of suckers.

Local Conditions

Chicago-Specific Considerations for Stump Grinding

Dense Urban Lots

Narrow city lots often mean the stump sits close to a fence, garage, or neighboring property line — our crews use compact grinders sized for exactly this kind of access.

Backyard Access Challenges

Many Chicago bungalows and two-flats have gated side yards with limited clearance. We assess access during the site visit so the right-sized equipment shows up the first time.

Utility Corridors

Underground gas, electric, and cable lines run through many Chicago yards. We review utility markings before grinding near any suspected line.

Parkway Trees

If the stump is in the parkway strip between the sidewalk and street, it may fall under the City of Chicago's Bureau of Forestry jurisdiction rather than private scope — we'll help you confirm which applies.

Clay Soil Conditions

Chicago's heavy clay soil compacts easily and drains poorly, which affects how quickly a ground-out site is ready for new sod or planting.

Mature Urban Canopy

Decades-old stumps from mature trees have larger, deeper root flares — these take more grinding time than a young tree removed after storm damage.

    Common Questions

    Stump Grinding FAQ

    Is stump grinding necessary?

    It isn't legally required, but it's strongly recommended. Left in place, a stump is a trip hazard, a pest magnet, and a barrier to replanting or landscaping — grinding is the step that actually finishes a tree removal project.

    How deep does stump grinding go?

    We typically grind 6–12 inches below the surrounding soil grade, which removes the root flare and the visible stump while leaving the site ready for backfill and replanting.

    How much does stump grinding cost in Chicago?

    Most residential stumps range from $150 to $900 depending on diameter, access, and root flare size. See our full stump grinding cost guide for a detailed breakdown.

    Can I plant a tree where a stump was removed?

    Yes, in most cases, once the site has been backfilled and given time to settle. Species selection and soil amendment matter — our arborists can recommend the right approach for your yard.

    Will roots continue growing after stump grinding?

    No. Once the stump and root flare are ground out, the tree cannot regrow. Remaining underground root sections decay naturally over time and, for some species, may send up temporary suckers until that process completes.

    How long does stump grinding take?

    Most single residential stumps are completed in one to two hours. Larger, mature stumps or multiple stumps on one property take longer and are scoped during the site inspection.

    Does stump grinding damage my yard?

    Impact is minimal and localized to the stump area itself. We use protective mats over lawns, driveways, and walkways to prevent damage from the equipment during the job.

    What happens to the wood chips?

    We can use the ground wood chips to help backfill the cavity, leave a pile for your own mulching use, or haul them away completely — whichever you prefer.

    Is stump grinding better than stump removal?

    For most residential properties, yes — it's faster, less expensive, and far less disruptive to the surrounding landscape than full excavation, while achieving the same practical result.

    Can old stumps attract insects?

    Yes. Decaying stumps are a common nesting site for carpenter ants and wood-boring beetles, which can spread to nearby healthy trees or structures if left untreated.
