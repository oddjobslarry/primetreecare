Why It Matters

Why Tree Planting Matters More Than Ever in Chicago

Chicago's tree canopy has taken two serious hits in the last two decades, and both are still shaping what a smart planting plan looks like today.

Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) has killed or forced the removal of a substantial share of the region's ash trees, which for decades were among the most commonly planted street and residential species in Chicagoland. That loss wasn't just cosmetic. Mature ash canopy provided shade that reduced cooling costs, intercepted stormwater, and supported neighborhood property values — all of which disappear block by block as dead ash trees come down. Property owners who replace a lost ash with nothing, or with another single vulnerable species, are setting up the next generation of canopy loss instead of solving it.

Beyond EAB, Chicago's urban forest faces compounding pressure: increasingly frequent high-wind storm events that snap weak-wood species, an aging tree population planted in waves during the same eras (meaning many trees are reaching structural decline together), historically limited species diversity in many neighborhoods, and the general stress of urban soils, road salt, compaction, and reflected heat from pavement and buildings.

This is exactly why species diversity is the foundation of every planting plan we design. Dutch Elm Disease wiped out the American Elm as Chicago's dominant street tree in the mid-20th century. Cities responded by leaning heavily on ash as the replacement — and Emerald Ash Borer proved that lesson had been forgotten. A resilient canopy needs a genuine mix of species, families, and growth habits, so that no single pest or disease can ever again take out a whole neighborhood's tree cover in one pass. Our planting recommendations are built around the 10-20-30 rule of urban forestry: no more than 10% of one species, 20% of one genus, or 30% of one family in a given planting area.

Understanding what happened with Emerald Ash Borer in Chicago and the earlier wave of Dutch Elm Disease is the starting point for any serious replanting strategy — both are covered in more depth on our dedicated resource pages.

Ash Replacement

What Should You Plant Instead of Ash Trees?

This is the single most common question we get from Chicago homeowners who lost a mature ash to Emerald Ash Borer.

Replacing a lost ash tree matters because that tree was likely providing meaningful shade, stormwater interception, and canopy structure that took decades to grow. Skipping replacement — or replacing it thoughtlessly — extends the canopy loss that EAB started.

Diversity is the whole point. Ash trees became a dominant Chicago species precisely because they were planted so heavily as replacements for the elms lost to Dutch Elm Disease. That decision is exactly why EAB was able to devastate so much of the region's canopy at once. Planting the same replacement species across a block or neighborhood — even a currently healthy one — sets up the next monoculture collapse. Our species recommendations intentionally spread risk across genus and family lines.

Site & Soil

Chicago Soil Conditions & Proper Site Selection

Clay soil challenges: Much of Cook County sits on dense, compacted clay that drains slowly and resists root penetration. Species selection and soil amendment both need to account for this from day one, or a newly planted tree will struggle for years.

Drainage considerations: Standing water around a root ball after heavy rain is one of the fastest ways to kill a newly planted tree. We evaluate drainage patterns before recommending final placement.

Root space requirements: Below-grade space is often more limiting than above-grade space, especially near sidewalks, driveways, and parkways. We plan for mature root spread, not just canopy spread.

Utility conflicts: Overhead power lines and underground utilities both restrict which species and locations are viable. We coordinate placement to avoid future utility trimming or conflicts.

Parkway tree considerations: Trees planted between the sidewalk and street are typically governed by the City of Chicago's Bureau of Forestry, with species, spacing, and permitting requirements that differ from private-property plantings.

Property line & easement considerations: Easements, right-of-way restrictions, and utility vegetation management zones can all limit where a tree may legally be planted.

Transplanting

Tree Transplanting Services

Sometimes the right tree is already on your property — it just needs to move. Transplanting can save an established tree instead of removing and starting over.

When transplanting makes sense: Construction relocation, correcting an original planting mistake, or saving a tree with sentimental or landscape value are the most common reasons Chicago property owners choose transplanting over removal.

Small tree relocation is the most reliable category — young trees under about 2 inches in trunk diameter transplant with a high success rate and minimal setback. Mature tree relocation is far more complex, requiring specialized equipment, a properly sized root ball, and realistic expectations about post-transplant stress.

Survival factors include tree species, age, root ball size relative to trunk diameter, soil moisture at the time of the move, and how quickly the tree is watered and monitored afterward. Best timing is generally late fall through early spring, while the tree is dormant and root disturbance causes the least stress.

Root ball preparation — root pruning several months before the move, when possible — significantly improves survival odds by encouraging a denser, more transportable root mass.

  • 02

  • 03

    Utility Clearance

Storm Resilience

Planting for Storm Resilience

Chicagoland has seen a documented rise in high-wind derecho and straight-line wind events in recent years — and species selection is your first line of defense.

Species selection: Weak-wood species like Silver Maple and Callery Pear fail at branch unions far more often than strong-wood species like Oak and Kentucky Coffeetree. We steer new plantings away from historically storm-prone species.

Structural development: Early structural pruning during a tree's first decade establishes a strong central leader and well-spaced scaffold branches — the biggest factor in whether a tree survives high wind decades later.

Wind resistance is influenced by canopy density, branch attachment angles, and root architecture. Root establishment in the first several years determines a tree's structural anchoring, which is why proper planting depth and watering schedules matter so much early on.

We design every planting plan with Chicago's exposure to derecho and straight-line wind events in mind — building long-term stability into the tree from the day it goes into the ground, not as an afterthought.

Aftercare

Newly Planted Tree Care Guide

The first two to three years after planting determine whether a tree thrives for decades or struggles indefinitely.

Watering

Deep, slow watering 1–2 times per week during the first growing season — more during hot, dry stretches — is the single biggest survival factor.

Mulching

A 2–3 inch mulch ring, kept off the trunk itself, conserves moisture and moderates soil temperature around new roots.

Fertilization

Most newly planted trees don't need fertilizer in year one — soil testing should guide any amendments, not a blanket schedule.

Pruning

Limit pruning in the first year to damaged or crossing branches only; structural pruning begins once the tree is established.

Monitoring

Check for leaning, girdling stakes, pest activity, and leaf stress regularly through the establishment period.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Planting too deep, over-mulching against the trunk ("mulch volcanoes"), and inconsistent watering are the top causes of early tree failure.

Common Questions

What Chicago Homeowners Ask Us Most

What is the best tree to plant in Chicago?

There isn't one single best tree — the right choice depends on your site, soil, and space — but resilient, well-adapted options for most Chicago properties include Bur Oak, Swamp White Oak, Kentucky Coffeetree, Hackberry, and disease-resistant elm cultivars. These species tolerate clay soil, urban stress, and Chicago's temperature swings better than many ornamental imports.

What should I replace an ash tree with?

Avoid replacing one ash with another ash, and avoid planting the same species as your neighbors to prevent a future monoculture. Strong replacements include Kentucky Coffeetree, Bur Oak, Swamp White Oak, Hackberry, Honey Locust, Ginkgo, and American Linden, chosen based on your site's sun, soil, and space.

When is the best time to plant trees in Chicago?

Early spring after the ground thaws and fall before the ground freezes are both strong planting windows in Chicago. Fall planting is often preferred for many species because cooler air and warm soil encourage root establishment before the stress of summer heat.

How much does tree planting cost?

Cost depends on tree size, species, site preparation, and accessibility. A small ornamental tree costs less than a large shade tree requiring soil amendment or crane placement. Prime Tree Care provides a free, written, on-site estimate before any work begins.

Can mature trees be transplanted?

Yes, but success depends heavily on tree size, species, root ball preparation, and timing. Smaller and younger trees transplant far more reliably than large, mature specimens. An arborist evaluation can set realistic expectations before you commit to a mature tree relocation.

How far should a tree be planted from a house?

As a general rule, large shade trees should be planted at least 15 to 20 feet from a foundation, while smaller ornamental trees can be planted closer, around 8 to 10 feet. The right distance depends on the species' mature canopy spread and root system.

What trees grow best in clay soil?

Bur Oak, Swamp White Oak, Hackberry, Kentucky Coffeetree, and Honey Locust are well suited to Chicago's dense clay soil because they tolerate compaction and variable drainage better than many shallow-rooted ornamental species.

Which trees resist Emerald Ash Borer?

Emerald Ash Borer only attacks true ash (Fraxinus) species, so any non-ash tree is inherently resistant. Strong alternatives include Kentucky Coffeetree, Bur Oak, Hackberry, Ginkgo, and Honey Locust, none of which host EAB.

How long does a newly planted tree take to establish?

As a general guideline, trees need roughly one year of establishment time per inch of trunk diameter. During this period, consistent watering and monitoring are critical to long-term survival.

Can an arborist help select the right tree?

Yes. An ISA-certified arborist evaluates your soil, sun exposure, drainage, utility lines, and long-term space constraints to recommend species that will actually thrive on your specific property, rather than a generic, one-size-fits-all suggestion.
