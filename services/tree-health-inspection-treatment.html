Why It Matters

Why Tree Health Inspections Matter in Chicago

Short answer: A professional inspection catches problems while treatment is still an option — often saving thousands of dollars in removal costs and protecting mature trees that took decades to grow.

Chicago's urban forest is under more pressure than most homeowners realize. Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) has moved through nearly every neighborhood in the city and suburbs, quietly killing ash trees from the inside out long before visible symptoms appear. Dutch Elm Disease continues to threaten the region's remaining American elms, and pockets of Oak Wilt show up wherever oaks are pruned or wounded during the growing season. Add in the structural weaknesses common to Silver Maples — a species planted heavily across Chicago decades ago — and the risk of sudden limb failure is real on almost every block.

It isn't only pests and disease. Chicago's heavy clay soil compacts easily and drains poorly, restricting root growth and starving trees of oxygen. Construction projects, sidewalk replacements, and utility work regularly damage root systems without anyone noticing until the canopy starts to thin a season or two later. Winter road salt splashes onto parkway trees and leaches into soil near driveways, while Chicagoland's increasingly frequent high-wind storms add mechanical stress that a weakened tree may not survive.

Most of these problems are diagnosable years before they become an emergency — but only if someone is looking for them. A certified arborist trained to recognize early-stage decline can often set a tree on a treatment path that costs a fraction of removal, preserves property value and shade coverage, and avoids the safety risk of a tree failing unexpectedly. Waiting until a tree looks obviously sick usually means the window for treatment has already closed.

Warning Signs

Signs Your Tree May Need Professional Inspection

If you're noticing any of the following, it's worth having a certified arborist take a closer look before the situation worsens.

1

Dead Branches

Branches with no leaves during the growing season often signal disease, root damage, or advanced pest activity.

2

Thinning Canopy

A canopy that looks sparse compared to previous years is one of the earliest visible signs of decline.

3

Premature Leaf Drop

Leaves falling well before autumn usually point to drought stress, root problems, or vascular disease.

4

Leaf Discoloration

Yellowing, browning, or unusual mottling can indicate nutrient deficiency, soil issues, or infection.

5

Bark Cracking

Vertical cracks or peeling bark can expose internal decay and are a common precursor to canker disease.

6

Fungal Growth

Mushrooms or conks at the base or trunk are a strong indicator of internal root or wood decay.

7

Leaning Trees

A new or worsening lean can mean root failure and is often an urgent safety concern.

8

Woodpecker Activity

Increased woodpecker feeding often means the tree already hosts a significant insect infestation, including EAB.

9

D-Shaped Exit Holes

Small D-shaped holes in the bark are the signature sign of Emerald Ash Borer.

10

Root Damage

Construction, trenching, or grade changes near the root zone can compromise stability and nutrient uptake.

11

Storm Injury

Broken limbs, split unions, or exposed wood after a storm need evaluation for infection risk and structural integrity.

12

Cavities & Decay

Hollow sections or soft, crumbling wood reduce a tree's structural strength and raise failure risk.

Chicago's #1 Tree Threat

Emerald Ash Borer Detection & Treatment Planning

Short answer: Emerald Ash Borer is an invasive beetle that has killed hundreds of millions of ash trees across North America, and Chicago's dense ash population has been hit especially hard. Caught early, many ash trees can be saved with insecticide treatment; caught late, removal is often the only safe option.

What Is Emerald Ash Borer?

Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) is a metallic-green invasive beetle whose larvae tunnel through the inner bark of ash trees, cutting off the flow of water and nutrients. Since arriving in the Chicago area, EAB has devastated the region's ash population — historically one of the most heavily planted street and residential trees in the city. Left undetected, EAB can kill a mature ash tree within two to four years of infestation.

Signs of EAB

  • D-shaped exit holes in the bark, roughly 1/8 inch wide
  • Bark splitting to reveal S-shaped larval galleries underneath
  • Increased woodpecker activity as birds feed on larvae
  • Epicormic shoots sprouting from the trunk or major limbs
  • Canopy thinning that typically starts at the top of the tree

Treat vs. Remove: The Decision Framework

Whether an ash tree can be saved comes down to how much canopy has already been lost and how structurally sound the tree remains. Here's how we generally advise homeowners:

Disease Diagnosis

Tree Diseases We Commonly Diagnose

Beyond EAB, our arborists regularly diagnose and treat these diseases across Chicago-area properties.

High Risk

Dutch Elm Disease

Symptoms: Wilting, yellowing leaves in a single branch or section, followed by rapid canopy dieback.

Treatment: Preventive fungicide injections for high-value elms; prompt removal of infected trees to stop beetle-borne spread.

High Risk

Oak Wilt

Symptoms: Leaves bronzing from the edges inward, sudden wilting in red oaks especially, rapid crown loss.

Treatment: Root graft disruption, fungicide injection for high-value oaks, and strict avoidance of pruning during active transmission season.

Moderate Risk

Anthracnose

Symptoms: Irregular brown blotches on leaves, premature leaf drop, twig dieback in wet springs.

Treatment: Sanitation pruning, canopy thinning for airflow, and fungicide in severe or recurring cases.

Moderate Risk

Bacterial Leaf Scorch

Symptoms: Leaf margins browning while the interior stays green, worsening yearly, slow canopy decline.

Treatment: No cure exists; we focus on stress reduction, monitoring, and extending the tree's usable lifespan.

High Risk

Root Rot

Symptoms: Thinning canopy, mushroom growth at the base, soft or crumbling wood near the root flare.

Treatment: Soil and drainage correction where possible; removal recommended once structural stability is compromised.

Manageable

Canker Diseases

Symptoms: Sunken, discolored patches on bark or branches, oozing sap, localized dieback.

Treatment: Pruning out affected wood, improving tree vigor, and monitoring for spread to healthy tissue.

Reading The Canopy

Canopy Decline Diagnosis

Short answer: Canopy decline is a gradual loss of leaf density, color, or size that signals the tree is under stress — and it's almost always caused by something happening below ground, not in the branches themselves.

What canopy decline means: Instead of a sudden event, decline develops over one or more growing seasons as smaller leaves, sparse foliage, and dead twigs accumulate. Because the symptoms show up in the canopy, homeowners often assume the problem is "in the leaves" — but the canopy is usually the last part of the tree to show a problem that started elsewhere.

Common causes include drought stress from Chicago's variable rainfall, soil compaction in high-traffic yards, root damage from construction or trenching, grade changes near the root flare, active disease, and insect pressure such as EAB. Often more than one factor is at work simultaneously.

Why early detection matters: A tree that's caught in early-stage decline usually responds well to soil correction, fertilization, or targeted treatment. Once decline reaches 50% or more of the canopy, recovery becomes far less likely and removal often becomes the safer, more cost-effective option.

Common Questions

Tree Health Inspection FAQs

How much does a tree inspection cost in Chicago?
Prime Tree Care provides free on-site assessments for most residential properties. More in-depth diagnostic work, such as lab sampling for disease confirmation, may carry an additional cost that we'll always explain upfront before proceeding.
Can an arborist tell if a tree is dying?
Yes. A certified arborist evaluates canopy density, bark condition, root flare, and pest or disease indicators to determine whether a tree is declining, stable, or actively dying — and how much time remains to intervene.
How do I know if I have Emerald Ash Borer?
Look for D-shaped exit holes in the bark, canopy thinning starting at the top of the tree, bark splitting over S-shaped larval tunnels, and increased woodpecker activity. An arborist inspection is the most reliable way to confirm EAB and determine whether treatment is still viable.
Can a diseased tree be saved?
Often, yes — especially when caught early. The right treatment depends on the specific disease, how much of the tree is affected, and its overall structural condition. Some diseases, like bacterial leaf scorch, have no cure but can be managed to extend the tree's life.
What causes canopy decline?
Common causes include drought stress, soil compaction, root damage from construction, grade changes, disease, and insect pressure. Canopy decline is usually a visible symptom of a problem happening below ground.
Should I remove or treat an ash tree?
It depends on canopy loss and structural condition. Trees with less than 30% canopy loss and no structural defects are generally strong candidates for treatment. Trees with more than 50% canopy loss or trunk decay are usually recommended for removal.
How often should trees be inspected?
We recommend an annual inspection for mature trees, and more frequent monitoring for trees already under treatment, showing early decline, or located near a home, driveway, or power line.
What is included in a tree health assessment?
A full assessment covers visual tree evaluation, canopy analysis, trunk and bark inspection, root zone evaluation, disease and pest identification, and a written treatment recommendation.
