Section 1

Why Chicago Tree Care Is Different

Generic tree-service descriptions don't hold up in a city with century-old parkway trees, strict public-vs-private ownership rules, and some of the most volatile storm swings in the Midwest. Here's what actually shapes tree care decisions in Chicago.

Mature Urban Canopy

Chicago's historic neighborhoods — Hyde Park, Lincoln Square, Beverly, and dozens of others — carry a canopy shaped by decades of dense residential development. Many parkway and yard trees are 60, 80, even over 100 years old, planted when lots were platted in the early 1900s. That mature canopy is a defining feature of these neighborhoods' character, but it also means an aging tree population that needs proactive risk management rather than reactive removal. Managing that aging canopy well means regular inspection, structural pruning to extend safe lifespan, and honest evaluation of which trees are candidates for preservation versus removal.

Parkway Trees vs. Private Trees

This is the single most common point of confusion we hear from Chicago homeowners. A parkway tree is planted in the strip of land between the sidewalk and the street. Despite sitting directly in front of a private home, parkway trees are owned and maintained by the City of Chicago's Bureau of Forestry — not the adjacent property owner. Homeowners cannot legally prune, treat, or remove a parkway tree on their own; requests for inspection, pruning, or removal go through the city, typically initiated via 311 or CHI311, Chicago's non-emergency service request system. Private trees — anything on your own lot outside the parkway strip — are entirely the homeowner's responsibility to maintain, insure, and remove as needed. For the complete breakdown with examples by neighborhood, see our dedicated guide: Parkway Trees vs. Private Trees in Chicago.

Chicago Weather Challenges

Few U.S. cities put trees through the range of stress Chicago's weather delivers across a single year. Winter ice accumulation adds significant weight to limbs, especially on trees already weakened by decay, and ice storms are a leading cause of large-limb failure between December and February. Spring storms bring the season's first heavy wind and rain after a dormant winter, catching trees at a vulnerable transition point. Summer thunderstorms and straight-line winds — sustained, non-rotating wind events that can exceed 60-70 mph — are Chicago's most common cause of sudden, catastrophic limb and trunk failure, and the region has seen an increasing number of derecho-type events in recent storm seasons, which combine hurricane-force gusts with widespread canopy damage over a large area. Drought stress in dry summer stretches weakens root systems and makes trees more vulnerable to the next wind event, creating a compounding risk pattern our arborists watch for year to year. For the full picture on Chicago's increasingly severe wind events, see Chicago Derecho Storm Damage to Trees.

Common Tree Problems Across Chicago

Four issues account for the large majority of disease- and pest-related tree loss across the city.

Emerald Ash Borer

Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) has killed a large share of Chicago's ash tree population since its arrival in the region. Watch for D-shaped exit holes in the bark, thin or yellowing canopy starting at the crown, and vertical bark splitting. The critical decision point is treatment vs. removal: trees caught with less than roughly 30% canopy loss often respond well to systemic insecticide treatment, while trees past that threshold are usually better candidates for removal before they become a structural hazard.

Emerald Ash Borer in Chicago →

Dutch Elm Disease

Dutch Elm Disease continues to affect Chicago's remaining mature elms, spread by elm bark beetles and through connected root systems between nearby trees. Symptoms include sudden wilting and yellowing of leaves on individual branches — "flagging" — often starting in the upper canopy in early summer. Management includes root-graft disruption between nearby elms, sanitation pruning, and in some cases preventative fungicide injection for high-value trees.

Dutch Elm Disease in Chicago →

Silver Maple Failures

Silver maple failure is one of the most frequently discussed tree problems in Chicago homeowner forums, and for good reason — the species' fast growth produces notably weak wood. Large limb loss during summer storms is common even on otherwise healthy-looking silver maples, and older specimens with included bark at major limb unions are especially prone to sudden, large-scale failure.

Silver Maple Problems in Chicago →

Oak Wilt Concerns

Oak wilt spreads both through root grafts between nearby oaks and via sap beetles attracted to fresh pruning wounds — which is why pruning timing matters so much for this species. Oaks should not be pruned between roughly April and July, when beetle activity peaks; dormant-season pruning in late fall through winter carries far lower disease-spread risk.

Removal cost is one of the most searched tree-care topics in the city, and the honest answer is: it depends primarily on size, with several variables shifting the total up or down.

Tree SizeTypical Range
Under 12"$300 – $800
Medium Trees$1,500 – $3,000
Large Mature Trees$3,500 – $6,000
Large Oaks$6,000 – $9,000+

Beyond size, the variables that move a quote are consistent citywide: diameter and height determine cutting and rigging time; crane requirement adds equipment cost but often reduces overall risk and project time on large or tightly positioned trees; access limitations — common on narrow North Side lots or alley-access South Side properties — can add labor time; power line proximity requires extra caution and sometimes utility coordination; disposal depends on total wood volume; and stump grinding is quoted separately from the removal itself. Signs You Need an Arborist

These are the indicators an ISA-certified arborist checks first during a tree risk assessment — often following the ISA's TRAQ (Tree Risk Assessment Qualification) framework for evaluating likelihood of failure against nearby targets.

1

Dead Branches

Brittle limbs with no live growth can fail without warning, even in light wind.

2

Leaning Trees

A new or worsening lean often signals root failure and elevated uproot risk.

3

Root Damage

Lifted soil, cut roots from construction, or exposed roots weaken anchoring.

4
Mushrooms at Base

Fungal conks at the root flare are a strong visible indicator of internal decay.

5

Bark Separation

Peeling or split bark exposes the tree to decay organisms and pests.

6

Repeated Branch Drop

A tree that has already dropped limbs is statistically likely to drop more.

7

Canopy Thinning

Sparse foliage relative to neighboring trees often points to root or disease stress.

8

Utility Conflicts

Limbs growing into power or service lines need professional, coordinated attention.

9

Cracks in Scaffold Limbs

Cracking in major structural limbs signals the union is close to failure.

Section 9

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does tree removal cost in Chicago?

Tree removal typically costs $300–$800 for trees under 12 feet, $1,500–$3,000 for medium trees, $3,500–$6,000 for large mature trees, and $6,000–$9,000+ for large oaks and other oversized trees requiring a crane. Diameter, height, access, crane needs, power line proximity, disposal, and stump grinding all affect the final price.

Do I need a permit to remove a tree in Chicago?

Private trees on your own property generally do not require a City of Chicago permit to remove. Parkway trees are owned by the city and are handled through the Bureau of Forestry, typically initiated via CHI311, rather than a private removal permit.

What is a parkway tree?

A parkway tree is a tree planted in the strip of land between the sidewalk and the street. In Chicago, these trees are owned and maintained by the Bureau of Forestry, not the adjacent homeowner, even though the tree sits directly in front of a private residence.

How often should trees be inspected?

Most mature Chicago trees benefit from a professional inspection every 2–3 years, with annual checks for older trees, trees near structures, or storm-prone species like silver maple. Trees showing warning signs should be inspected immediately.

What trees fail most often during storms?

Silver maple, cottonwood, and willow are among the most storm-prone species in Chicago due to fast growth and weaker wood. Ash trees weakened by Emerald Ash Borer and elms affected by Dutch Elm Disease are also more prone to failure since disease damage compromises structural integrity before visible collapse.

What is Emerald Ash Borer?

Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) is an invasive beetle that has killed a large share of Chicago's ash population. Larvae tunnel under the bark and disrupt water and nutrient flow, causing canopy thinning, D-shaped exit holes, and eventual death if untreated.

Is tree trimming better than tree removal?

Trimming is preferable whenever a tree is structurally sound and the problem can be corrected with pruning. Removal becomes the right call when a tree has irreversible decay, major structural failure, or disease that has progressed too far for treatment.

What does an ISA-certified arborist do?

An ISA-certified arborist has passed the International Society of Arboriculture's exam covering tree biology, risk assessment, pruning standards, and safe work practices, and applies that training to diagnose and treat, prune, or remove trees safely.

Who is responsible for a fallen tree?

In most cases, each property owner is responsible for damage on their own property regardless of whose tree caused it, and homeowner insurance typically covers removal when a tree damages a covered structure. Liability can shift if the tree owner had documented prior notice of a hazard.

When is the best time to prune trees in Chicago?

Late winter through early spring, while trees are dormant, is generally best for structural pruning. Oaks should avoid pruning between roughly April and July to reduce oak wilt risk, and elms should avoid peak bark beetle season to limit Dutch Elm Disease transmission.
