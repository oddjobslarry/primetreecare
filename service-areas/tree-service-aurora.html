Local Risks

Common Tree Challenges in Aurora

Emerald Ash Borer Losses

Aurora's Forestry Division manages roughly 35,000 city street trees, a share of which are ash lost or declining to EAB over the past decade. We assess whether trunk-injection treatment can still save a tree or whether removal and species-diversified replanting is the better long-term investment.

Storm Damage Risks

Straight-line winds, summer derecho-style events, and heavy wet snow regularly snap poorly thinned limbs across Aurora's older canopy. Preventive structural pruning and cabling before storm season is far cheaper than emergency removal after.

Aging Shade Trees

Legacy oaks and silver maples around Stolp Island and Stonebridge often carry co-dominant stems, decay pockets, and root stress from decades of construction, sidewalk work, and compacted urban soil — all findings we document in a full risk assessment.

Know the Rules Before You Cut: Aurora's Forestry Division

The City of Aurora's Parks, Recreation & Open Space Department – Division of Forestry maintains the roughly 35,000 trees planted in public rights-of-way, generally within five feet of sidewalks or in the planting strip between sidewalk and street. It's illegal for residents to remove or damage a city tree, and only city-licensed arborists may perform city-tree work under a Forestry Division permit. Private trees, everything fully on your own lot, are your responsibility to maintain and remove. Aurora's Unified Development Ordinance also applies additional tree-preservation and permitting requirements for larger removals and new development, administered through the City Forester's office. We help Aurora property owners tell the difference and navigate whichever process applies.

Tree Species

Common Tree Species in Aurora

Oak Trees

Aurora's most valuable legacy shade trees around Stolp Island and older east-side blocks. We use dormant-season structural pruning and preservation-first plans, avoiding any fresh wounds during oak-wilt season (April–June).

Maple Trees

Mature silver and sugar maples throughout older neighborhoods are prone to storm damage from weak branch unions. Proactive limb management and cabling reduce failure risk before a wind event forces the issue.

Ash Trees

Once common street and yard plantings, Aurora's remaining ash trees face ongoing Emerald Ash Borer pressure. We evaluate each tree individually for treatment viability versus removal and replacement.

Honey Locust

A common, urban-tolerant street and parkway species around commercial corridors and mid-century subdivisions. Requires regular structural maintenance to manage its tendency toward included bark and weak crotches.

Elm Trees

Surviving American elms and newer disease-resistant cultivars around older Aurora blocks need ongoing Dutch elm disease monitoring, since the fungus spreads quickly through root grafts and bark-beetle activity.

Newer Subdivision Plantings

Far east and west side developments favor hackberry, swamp white oak, hophornbeam, and Kentucky coffeetree — species we recommend precisely because they diversify Aurora's canopy away from single-species risk.

Common Questions

Aurora Tree Service FAQ

How much does tree service cost in Aurora?+

Most Aurora homeowners pay $300–$1,500 for trimming, $300–$3,500 for small to medium tree removal, and $4,000+ for large or crane-assisted removals. Stump grinding runs $150–$1,200 depending on diameter and root spread. Final pricing depends on tree size, accessibility, and proximity to structures or utility lines.

What tree species are most common in Aurora?+

Aurora's canopy is dominated by mature oaks and maples in older neighborhoods like Stolp Island and the Near Eastside, honey locust and remaining ash plantings from mid-century subdivisions, and younger species-diverse plantings in newer far-east and far-west developments.

Should I remove or treat an ash tree with Emerald Ash Borer?+

It depends on infestation severity. Trees with canopy loss under 30% and no major trunk damage are often good candidates for trunk-injection treatment. Trees with significant dieback, bark splitting, or structural decay are usually safer and more cost-effective to remove and replace.

When is the best time to prune trees in Aurora?+

Late winter — roughly February through March — is the ideal dormant-season window for most structural pruning. Oaks specifically should be pruned only in this dormant window to avoid oak wilt, which spreads through sap-feeding beetles most active April through June.

Can storm-damaged trees be saved?+

Often, yes. A tree with less than 50% crown loss, an intact leader, and no major trunk splitting can usually be pruned back and supported with cabling. Trees with split trunks, exposed root plates, or damage to the main leader are typically safety risks and require removal.

What is a tree risk assessment?+

A tree risk assessment is an ISA-certified arborist's structured evaluation of a tree's species, structure, decay, root stability, and proximity to targets like homes, driveways, or power lines, resulting in a documented risk rating and a recommended treatment, monitoring, or removal plan.

How often should mature trees be inspected?+

Mature shade trees in established Aurora neighborhoods should be inspected annually, with an additional check after any major storm. Trees showing early decline, prior storm damage, or known EAB exposure benefit from twice-yearly monitoring.

What should I plant instead of ash trees in Aurora?+

Strong replacement choices for Aurora's soil and climate include hackberry, swamp white oak, disease-resistant elm cultivars, hophornbeam, and Kentucky coffeetree. Diversifying species across a block reduces the risk of losing an entire street's canopy to a single pest or disease again.
