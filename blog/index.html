Why This Hub Exists

Tree Care Challenges Are Different in Chicago

Most tree-care advice online is written for a national audience — and it shows. It rarely accounts for the specific conditions that shape tree ownership and tree health here in Chicago.

Chicago's urban forest carries scars and pressures a generic blog post won't mention: the ongoing fallout from Emerald Ash Borer, which has removed tens of thousands of ash trees from parkways and yards citywide; Dutch Elm Disease, which continues to threaten the city's remaining elms; and a mature urban canopy planted in waves during the mid-20th century that is now aging into the stage of life where structural risk becomes real. Add to that the near-universal confusion over parkway tree ownership — many homeowners assume the tree between the sidewalk and the street is theirs, when it's often the City's — plus the layers of Bureau of Forestry process, permitting, and 311 requests that come with anything touching public-way trees.

On top of all that, Chicago sees genuine high-wind events and derechos most national guides never mention, historic neighborhoods with tree preservation considerations, frequent utility conflicts between mature canopy and overhead lines, and an aging population of silver maples prone to structural failure. Generic advice can't account for any of it. This Resource Center is built specifically around Chicago and Chicagoland conditions, written by the ISA-certified arborists who work in this canopy every day.

Common Questions

Chicago Tree Care FAQ

What are the most common tree problems in Chicago?

Emerald Ash Borer, Dutch Elm Disease, storm damage from high winds, root compaction in clay soil, and structural weaknesses in aging silver maples are among the most common issues we see across Chicago and the surrounding suburbs.

How do I know if a tree is city-owned?

Trees growing in the parkway — the strip of grass between the sidewalk and the street — are generally owned and maintained by the City of Chicago's Bureau of Forestry, not the adjacent homeowner. Trees fully within a private yard are typically the homeowner's responsibility.

What tree diseases are common in Chicago?

Dutch Elm Disease, oak wilt, and the ongoing impact of Emerald Ash Borer are the three most significant disease and pest pressures affecting Chicago's urban forest today.

When is the best time to prune trees?

Late fall through early spring — the dormant season — is generally best for most species, since it reduces disease transmission risk. Oaks in particular should avoid pruning during peak oak wilt season in late spring and summer.

Do I need a permit to remove a tree?

It depends on the tree's location. Parkway and other public-way trees require a Bureau of Forestry permit, while private trees generally have different requirements. Confirming a tree's ownership and status is the first step.

How often should trees be inspected?

We generally recommend a professional risk assessment every one to two years for mature trees, and immediately after any major storm event.

What should I do after storm damage?

Stay clear of any tree with visible cracks, hanging limbs, or contact with power lines, and call for emergency tree service immediately if a tree is threatening a structure, vehicle, or roadway.

What trees should replace ash trees?

Species like Kentucky coffeetree, swamp white oak, honey locust, and other diverse, climate-resilient natives are commonly recommended to replace lost ash trees and reduce future disease vulnerability.
